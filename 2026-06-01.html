The Agent Stack Matures: Memory, Outcomes, and Multi-Agent Orchestration

Builders across the ecosystem are converging on a new agent architecture: persistent memory, explicit outcome rubrics, and multi-agent delegation. Anthropic's Claude Managed Agents shipped dreaming (cross-session memory refinement), outcomes (self-grading against rubrics), and multi-agent orchestration — with Harvey, Netflix, Spiral, and Wisedocs reporting dramatic gains in completion rates and quality. Meanwhile, Peter Steinberger and Dan Shipper are pushing the same ideas from the practitioner side: longer-running prompts, agentic review loops, and treating 'yielding agents' as a learnable skill. The stack is shifting from 'one-shot prompt engineering' to 'managed agent systems.'

Official blogs

Claude Blog

Anthropic Engineering blog

Anthropic launched three major capabilities in Claude Managed Agents. Dreaming is a scheduled process that reviews past agent sessions, extracts patterns, and curates memories so agents improve over time — either automatically or with human review. Outcomes let developers define success rubrics; a separate grader evaluates agent output against those criteria and triggers self-correction loops. In internal benchmarks, outcomes improved task success by up to 10 points, with +8.4% on docx and +10.1% on pptx generation. Multi-agent orchestration lets a lead agent delegate to specialist subagents with their own models, prompts, and tools, all working on a shared filesystem. Early adopters include Harvey (legal drafting, ~6x completion rate improvement), Netflix (parallel log analysis across hundreds of builds), Spiral by Every (writing agent with editorial rubrics), and Wisedocs (document quality checks, 50% faster reviews).

Together, memory and dreaming form a robust memory system for self-improving agents.
→ https://claude.com/blog/new-in-claude-managed-agents

Oriol Vinyals

Co-lead of Gemini, Google DeepMind

The Takeaway: Google is betting that world models — not just language — are the path to AGI, and the 'GPT moment for video' hasn't happened yet. Oriol Vinyals, co-lead of Gemini, argues that while language models have distilled humanity's written knowledge, the visual world contains vast untapped information. The core challenge is representation learning: extracting concepts like gravity from raw pixels without relying on text labels. Google's new Omni model represents progress — it can input and output video, edit it via language, and simulate movements — but Vinyals is candid that pure visual understanding remains an open research problem. He also draws a direct line between world models and robotics: better simulation could accelerate robot training, though precision in motor control and physical forces remains a gap.

Could we train on all the videos ever produced and images and get to the same level of understanding that clearly the language models using language get to?
